# Week 20 — May 18

## AI Learning: DuckDB & Vectorized/Embedded OLAP Databases

Prompted by AWS's August 2026 acquisition of DuckLabs, worked through an interactive first-principles session on why DuckDB is architecturally distinct from both traditional row-store databases (Postgres) and other OLAP engines (ClickHouse, Snowflake, BigQuery). Covered six linked ideas: why columnar storage wins for analytical scan queries but loses for OLTP row-touching workloads; the three ways a query engine can execute (row-at-a-time Volcano model, full JIT compilation, and DuckDB's vectorized batch execution) and why ~1024–2048-row batches are the sweet spot between overhead amortization and CPU cache residency; why "vectorized execution" alone doesn't differentiate DuckDB (ClickHouse and Snowflake also use it) — the real differentiator is combining it with an embedded, zero-copy, in-process deployment model borrowed from SQLite; morsel-driven parallelism as dynamic work-stealing across CPU cores, and why `GROUP BY`/`JOIN` act as synchronization "pipeline breakers" requiring a local-aggregate-then-merge pattern; the actual scaling ceiling (in-memory working-set size for pipeline breakers, and single-writer concurrency) versus the graceful degradation of simply running out of cores; and finally how these properties combine to make DuckDB a natural fit for agentic AI workloads — zero-config ephemeral instances, zero-copy handoff of results back into an agent's reasoning loop, and direct querying of Parquet/S3 without an ETL step.

## Build

Built a distributed data-processing pipeline with [Ray](../tools/ray.md): read a 10M-line `.jsonl` file of `{"word": "..."}` records and add a `"length"` field to each, in six stages — sequential baseline (13.48s), parallel Ray tasks split across CPU cores (2.35s — 5.7x speedup, not 10x, traced to the Apple M4's heterogeneous performance/efficiency core split), a stateful `StatsActor` computing the average word length (8.0012) with zero locking due to Ray's serialized actor method calls, and a fault-tolerance exploration showing `max_retries` fixes transient failures but not deterministic bad data.
